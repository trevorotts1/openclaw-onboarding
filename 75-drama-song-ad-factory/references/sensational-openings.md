# Sensational openings — reference and teaching

Status: reference doctrine, both distributions (Skill 75 / 999 twin).
Used by: the opening / backstory step of the twelve-stage arc
(`references/drama-to-demand-method.md`, stage 2 and the `BASELINE` row),
the lyrics planner, and the shot plan's first three shots.
Companion: `references/opening-proposals.md` (worked candidate sets).

## 1. What an opening is for

The opening buys the next five seconds. It is not context, not a brand
introduction and not a mood piece. It puts a specific person inside a
consequence that has already happened, and leaves one question unanswered.

Three things must be true before the fourth second ends:

1. Something bad is already in progress.
2. The viewer knows whose life it is.
3. The viewer does not know how it ends or who caused it.

## 2. Start inside the consequence

The opening line begins in the middle of one of these, never before it:

| Crisis type | The moment you open on |
|---|---|
| Revelation | A document, result or message that changes what a character believed |
| Confrontation | Two people in the same room, one of them holding proof |
| Discovery | Finding the thing that was hidden — account, signature, file, body |
| Humiliation | Being seen in the failure, by someone whose opinion counts |
| Reversal | The thing that was supposed to be safe turning against them |
| Catastrophe | The material loss, on the floor, already done |

If the opening needs a sentence of setup before the crisis ("A few years ago,
things got hard for…"), the opening is one sentence too late. Cut the setup
and start on the crisis; background can be recovered later in dialogue, a
single object, or a lyric line.

## 3. Never open on these

- A logo, a title card, a brand name, a phone number.
- A biography: name, age, marital status, job title.
- Scenery: skyline, sunrise, waves, drone shot, traffic.
- A business introduction: "We're a family-owned company…"
- A generic question with no stake: "Are you tired of…?"
- A statistic with no person attached.
- A voice saying "Let me tell you my story."

Any of those in shot one is a rejection, not a preference. See §8 for worked
rejections.

## 4. First visible action

The first action is what the camera sees while (or just before) the opening
line lands. It must be **one unambiguous physical action that shows the
consequence**, not a scene that describes a situation.

- Yes: a hand flattening a denial letter on a counter; a couch leg snapping in
  wet grass; a phone held out so the other person reads the screen; a thumb
  pressing record on a voicemail for the fourth time.
- No: packing boxes; looking worried; walking down a hallway; a wide shot of a
  house.

The action carries the shock. The line carries the question. Neither may do
both — if the line explains the action, the line is doing a caption's job.

## 5. The six fields — every candidate, every time

A proposal candidate is not a headline plus a vibe. Each candidate is written
with all six, in this order:

| Field | What it must be |
|---|---|
| `opening_line` | The exact spoken line, word for word, as it will be performed. No stage direction inside it. |
| `headline` | The on-screen text. Short, concrete, states the damage, not the offer. |
| `first_action` | One physical action the camera lands on first. |
| `question` | The one question the opening makes the viewer ask. Written as a question. |
| `why_this_audience` | The specific stake for this audience — what they are afraid is already true about them. |
| `offer_link` | The causal chain from this crisis to what the offer actually does. Not "and then we helped" — the mechanism. |

Missing any field is an incomplete candidate and is not scored.

## 6. Genuinely different, not three rewrites

Three candidates are different when they differ in **crisis type, scene and
question**, not in adjective. If all three could be played by the same actor in
the same room, they are one candidate written three times. Reject the set.

- Different: a revelation at a bank desk, a humiliation in a driveway, a
  discovery on a phone call.
- Not different: "They denied me", "They rejected me", "They turned me down."

## 7. Recommendation rule

Score each candidate 0–5 on shock, curiosity and relevance to the offer, state
the three numbers, and recommend the highest total.

**Recommend the strongest candidate. Never default to the mildest.** A safe
opening that everyone can live with is the failure mode this file exists to
prevent. If the strongest candidate cannot be made compliant (§7.1), say
exactly what blocked it and recommend the next one — do not quietly slide to
the soft version.

Vetoes that override the score:

- The invented event is presented as a verified fact about a real person or
  real product (§7.2).
- The crisis has no credible mechanism into the offer (`offer_link` is
  decorative).
- The opening is carried by a claim the client cannot substantiate and the ad
  presents it as substantiated.

### 7.1 Invented catastrophe is permitted in drama

There is **no blanket rule** here prohibiting invented catastrophic events in
fictional or composite drama. This doctrine supersedes any such reading:

- A fictional or composite character may lose a job, a house, a marriage, a
  business, a case, a tooth, a roof, a parent. The story may invent an
  eviction, a denial, a foreclosure, a recall, a malpractice, a betrayal, a
  collapse. None of that is off-limits for being too severe.
- The prohibition in `drama-to-demand-method.md` on inventing "business facts,
  customer results, prices, credentials, deadlines, or testimonials" governs
  **claims about the real offer**. It does not govern plot events in a drama.
  Do not stretch it into a ban on the crisis.
- The mildness reflex — softening the event so nobody could object — produces
  the rejected openings in §8 and is not compliance. It is the defect.

### 7.2 The boundary that does not move

Invented events and invented outcomes are never presented as verified facts
about a real person or a real product.

- Characters are composite and unnamed beyond a first name. No real client,
  employee, doctor, officer or official is named or depicted as the villain.
- No on-screen label over fiction may say "verified", "real customer",
  "actual results", "studies show", or a specific measured outcome attributed
  to a real business.
- No invented review, testimonial, case result, award, licence or statistic.
  If the offer needs a number, it comes from the client and is marked with its
  source; otherwise the ad does not use one.
- The planning artifact labels each invented plot event `FICTIONAL_PLOT_EVENT`
  and each invented character `COMPOSITE`, and each client-supplied true story
  `CLIENT_SUPPLIED_TRUE`. The label travels with the beat into the lyrics and
  the shot plan.
- The offer's own claims stay exactly what the client authorised. A dramatic
  crisis may be fictional; the promise of the product may not be.

The test: could a viewer reasonably conclude that a named real person or a
named real business did the invented thing? If yes, the opening fails §7.2
regardless of its score.

## 8. Counterexamples — tame openings that were rejected

Each row is a real proposal shape this doctrine rejects, with the reason and
the repair direction. Use these as the teaching set; an agent that produces
one of these has not read §2.

| # | Rejected opening | Why it fails | Repair direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Hi, I'm Dana, founder of ClearPath Credit." | Biography plus logo. The viewer has no stake and no question. | Open at the denial, in the chair, with the letter in hand. Dana's name can appear later. |
| 2 | "Are you tired of bad credit holding you back?" | Generic question, no person, no consequence, interchangeable with any ad in the category. | Name one consequence already happening to one person. |
| 3 | "In today's economy, millions of Americans are struggling with debt." | Statistic billboard. No face, no moment, no question. | Replace the statistic with one scene that shows the same thing. |
| 4 | "Beautiful sunrise over the city. Soft piano." | Scenery. Nothing has happened and nothing is at risk. | Cut. Frame one is the crisis. |
| 5 | "Meet Sarah. She's a busy mom of three trying to rebuild." | Biography. The crisis arrives after the résumé, so the first four seconds are spent on a stranger's paperwork. | Sarah is revealed by what she is holding, not by her biography. |
| 6 | "We're a family-owned business serving homeowners since 1998." | Business introduction. It answers a question nobody asked yet. | Open on the homeowner's ceiling coming down; the company earns its introduction later. |
| 7 | Three rewrites of one tame line: "I had some credit trouble a while back" / "My credit wasn't great, honestly" / "Things got a little difficult financially." | One candidate in three costumes. Same crisis type, same scene, same intensity, no consequence, no question. | Replace the whole set with three different crisis types (§6). |
| 8 | "I lost my house." (said calmly, over a moving-box montage) | The event is strong but the moment is past tense and the action describes rather than shows. No specifics, so no picture. | Put it in the room where the notice is read, and let one object carry the loss. |

## 9. Interaction with the music and singing rules

This doctrine does not license an earlier vocal. The instrumental opening and
the no-singing-before-the-ERM rule in `drama-to-demand-method.md` still hold:
the opening line is **spoken** (or narrated), the first sung line arrives at the
pain apex, and no solution chorus, hummed hook or sung brand line is smuggled
into the opening. A sensational opening is a spoken and visual event.

The ~25-second opening ceiling is unchanged. A crisis that needs longer to
explain is not a stronger crisis; it is a later scene.

## 10. Reviewer checks

A checker answering the opening section reports numbers, not adjectives:

1. Does line one begin inside a crisis, with no setup sentence? (yes / no, first
   five words quoted)
2. Is frame one a physical action showing a consequence? (describe it in one
   sentence; "wide shot"/"logo"/"scenery" is a fail)
3. Is the question written down and singular? (quote it; two questions is a
   fail)
4. Is the crisis type named for each of the three candidates, and are all three
   different? (list the three types; duplicates fail the set)
5. Is `offer_link` a mechanism, not a transition? (quote the chain)
6. Is every invented event labelled `FICTIONAL_PLOT_EVENT` / `COMPOSITE`, and is
   no invented event presented as a verified fact about a real person or
   product? (list labels; any real attribution fails)
7. Does the recommended candidate carry the highest score, or is a written veto
   named? (three scores + recommendation shown)
